A little about me

Rachel Gross

ICI Postdoctoral Fellow · University of Virginia

I work with Prof. Ilse Cleeves and Prof. Catherine Dukes at the University of Virginia, where I received my Ph.D. in Chemistry in May 2026.

At the center of my research are astrophysical ices: what they contain, how their composition and structure change with their surroundings, and when molecules desorb into the gas as stars and planets form. Gas- and ice-phase chemistry are closely connected, so I also study the molecules in the gas and the disk conditions that shape their distributions.

I bring together cryogenic laboratory experiments, astronomical spectroscopy, chemical modeling, and radiative transfer. A recurring question across these projects is how to distinguish changes in the underlying chemistry from changes in the way we observe it.

What I'm working on

Current Research

My current projects connect gas and ice chemistry to the physical conditions of planet-forming environments and to the spectra we can recover from them.

Ongoing · Radiative transfer

When does disk ice leave an observable signature?

A chemical model tells us where molecules are in a disk, but a telescope measures light, not the underlying molecular inventory. The observable ice bands depend on geometry, scattering, optical depth, and which part of the disk contributes to an extracted spectrum.

I generate synthetic images and spectra of edge-on disks exposed to different external UV fields and compare the recovered H2O, CO2, and CO ice signatures with the model's physical abundances. I am also extending this work toward Orion silhouette-disk geometries, where nebular background light passes through the disk and provides a different view of its ice.

In development · Laboratory astrochemistry

How does CO2 ice structure affect its spectrum?

My current laboratory work is developing experiments to test how CO2 ice porosity influences its infrared spectral properties and the abundances inferred from those spectra, motivated in part by observations of CO2 on Ganymede.

This project builds on my experience developing, operating, and troubleshooting cryogenic ultra-high-vacuum instrumentation. I am also contributing to the laboratory's relocation and re-establishment in a new facility.

Published Research

Three examples from my doctoral work illustrate how laboratory measurements, JWST spectra, and gas–ice chemical models address different parts of the same larger picture.

Laboratory experiments · 2026

Why does the ice beneath methyl formate matter?

Methyl formate is an organic molecule detected in star-forming regions, but its desorption cannot be described by considering the molecule alone. I used temperature-programmed desorption experiments to investigate methyl formate deposited on water- and methanol-rich ice substrates, comparing how the substrate and coverage change its desorption behavior.

We found that methanol-rich ice mantles lower methyl formate binding energies relative to water-rich substrates. This provides experimentally grounded constraints for models that determine when complex organics move from icy grains into the gas.

JWST spectroscopy · CORINOS IV

How confidently can we measure a weak ice feature?

Weak molecular ice bands in JWST spectra often overlap with stronger absorptions. In CORINOS IV, I analyzed the 7–8 μm region of four Class 0 protostars, focusing on the blended SO2 feature and the uncertainties introduced by the choice of continuum.

I developed a Monte Carlo baseline-fitting approach that samples more than 50 continuum realizations per source, then combines the resulting optical-depth spectra with laboratory ice components. The study shows why uncertainty in continuum placement must be accounted for when deriving abundances from weak ice bands.

Chemical modeling · 2025

What changes when a disk forms near massive stars?

Not all planet-forming disks experience the extreme irradiation found near the centers of massive clusters. To examine the more common, moderately irradiated environments, I modeled T Tauri disks under external UV fields from 1 to 100 times the typical interstellar field, varying their outer disk structure and predicting synthetic molecular-line emission.

The key result is that the response is both molecule- and location-dependent. External UV creates an ionization front and changes the chemistry of the outer disk, with C+, N2H+, atomic C, and CS responding more strongly than CO and HCO+ in these models. More extended, less-shielded disks show a larger response, while the chemistry within roughly the inner 25 au remains comparatively unchanged.

These differences help identify molecular tracers of a disk's birth environment without assuming that external irradiation alters the chemistry of the entire disk uniformly.
